## 3. Material change — the energy-upgrade thesis weakens

This is the finding that actually moves a number.

| Element | Energielabel (2020, oude methodiek) | Vragenlijst Deel B (30-07-2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Gevel | **Not** insulated | **Insulated during construction, fully** (2c) — material unknown, no certificate |
| Dak | **Not** insulated | **Insulated during construction, 1984, fully** (3e) — "geen idee" which material |
| Vloer | Not insulated | **Not** insulated (5f) — ✔ both agree |

Who is right matters financially. A 1985 new-build fell under the post-1975 Bouwbesluit requirement of Rc ≥ 1.3, so the seller's version is **plausible** — old-methodology labels routinely score elements as uninsulated when no documentary proof is produced. But the answer comes from a representative who never lived in the house and holds no certificate, so it is not proof either.

**Consequence:** the round-1 assumption of a cheap C→A/B path leaned on cavity-wall insulation (~€2k, 3–5 yr payback — the single best-value measure in the Dutch stock). If the cavity is already filled, that win does not exist. The remaining route is floor insulation + HR++ glazing in wooden frames + PV ± hybrid heat pump: still roughly €20–30k, but without the quick wins.

→ **`energy_upgrade` reclassified `easy` → `moderate`** in the database (score effect: +1.0 → +0.5 on the energy criterion).
→ **Action:** have the bouwkundig keurder borescope the cavity (a 10-minute check through a ventilation opening). If it is empty, the classification reverts to `easy` and ~€2k buys a genuine step up.
